How cities are learning to live with heat

On summer afternoons, streets and buildings absorb energy from the sun and release it slowly after dark. This effect can make dense neighbourhoods several degrees warmer than surrounding countryside.

Urban planners are experimenting with trees, reflective roofs and shaded walkways. Trees are especially valuable because they provide shade while releasing water vapour through their leaves. However, planting programmes work only when cities choose species suited to the local climate and provide enough water during their early years.

Some cities have also created cool corridors that connect homes, schools and transport hubs. Rather than treating heat as a problem for individual buildings, these routes allow residents to move through an entire neighbourhood with less exposure.

Researchers warn that no single measure will be sufficient. The most successful plans combine changes to buildings, public space and emergency services, while prioritising neighbourhoods whose residents are most vulnerable.
